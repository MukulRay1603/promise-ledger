Roadmap

Where it helps, what’s different, and what’s next

Promises made for public support are easy to make and hard to follow. This page shows the moments where Promise Ledger helps, how it compares with what people use today, what we have measured, and where we are going by the Dec 2 final.

Why it matters

The problem is already on the record

5 records, 7 terms

A single deal’s promise can be spread across a staff summary, a letter, slides, minutes and an agreement. Checking 7 terms by hand across 5 records is 35 look-ups, before anyone compares them.

What’s different

Promise by promise, with the page as proof

  • Promises, not documents. The unit is one promise, such as “12 homes” or “30 years”, followed across every record of a deal.
  • Proof, not paraphrase. Every claim is an exact quote that code finds on its page. If it can’t be found, it isn’t shown.
  • The whole life of a deal. Each promise carries the stage it reached: proposed, supported, signed or delivered, and who signed.

How it works

The model is one step of eight

AI reads the wording, code checks it, and people make every decision.

  1. CodeRead pagesText from each PDF page; scans are flagged
  2. CodeRedactID numbers, emails and phone numbers removed
  3. AIProposeGemini suggests quotes, terms, pages and signers
  4. CodeVerifyEach quote must be on its page, word for word
  5. CodeSet stageRules, never the model alone
  6. CodeCompareChanged and missing terms across records
  7. PeopleDecideConfirm, follow up or dispute, with a note
  8. OutputShareReceipt, read-only link, Excel and reminders

What these are

Our development set: 3 made-up deals, 11 PDFs and 53 claims we labeled by hand, scored with evals/run_pdf_evals.py. The rules were tuned on the same set, and the sample PDFs are 1 to 4 pages, so cost grows with longer records.

Not measured yet

  • A held-out set, labeled blind
  • Real public records
  • Scanned pages and long packets
  • Time saved for real staff

Built with care

Who could be hurt by a wrong flag, and what we do about it

  • Developers and staff. A wrong “differs” could embarrass someone in public. Labels stay neutral, nothing is a legal finding, and a person decides every item.
  • What the model sees. Only redacted page text. The redactor catches ID numbers, emails and phone numbers, not names or addresses, so the demo asks for made-up records only.
  • Data use. Demo uploads are deleted after 24 hours. A real deployment should use the paid Gemini API or Vertex AI, whose terms don’t use prompts to train models.

What’s next

From working demo to a tool a housing office can use

  1. Working today

    • Reads a deal’s PDFs and finds each promise, with its exact quote and page
    • Code checks every quote word for word and sets each stage from rules
    • Shows what changed between records and which terms are missing
    • Flags one-party signing, missing certificates and terms about to end
    • Reviewers confirm, question or dispute each item, with their name and a note
    • One-page receipt, read-only share link, Excel and CSV export
    • Keyboard, screen reader, larger text and dark mode

  2. By the Dec 2 final

    • Accuracy on records it was never tuned on, reported apart from today’s set
    • At least one real public record, read end to end
    • Reads more kinds of records, including scans
    • Helps staff act on open questions, with a person approving every step
    • Feedback and a timed task with people who do this work
    • Staff accounts with roles, so a review follows the reviewer

  3. After that

    • More record types and more cities
    • A plain-language view for residents
    • A hand-off kit so a city or non-profit can run it on their own
